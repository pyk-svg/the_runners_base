The Beginner Guide

Introduction

Let me be clear - this guide isn’t for true and absolute couch potatoes. At this level, you should've had previous experience in other sports, or have run casually and consistently already. You want to get into structured training for a race, but don’t know where to start.

Fueling, Sleep, and Recovery

Training as a runner doesn’t just mean running. It means proper fueling and sleep. Think about recovery this way: the training is like a construction manager telling his workers to build a house, the food you eat are the bricks the house is built with, and sleep is the amount of time the workers get to build the house. Build the bricks out of chips and popcorn? The house is going to crumble. But build it out of generally healthy foods? Your house will be stable. One thing many beginners overlook is not getting enough fueling - more training = more food needed. Make sure you have enough bricks, and you’ll be on your way to building a good house. But that’s not the only component. The other half is sleep. If your workers don’t have time to build the house, they’re going to rush and it’s not going to be a great job. If they never get a consistent time to start and stop working, they also won’t do a great job. So make sure you’re sleeping at least 8 hours, at around the same time each night. Science favors higher numbers (9-10) which is great if you can achieve it, but in practice, with studies, high sleep latency, and other commitments, I’ve come to view 8-9 hours as a more realistic target.

Common Mistakes

The #1 mistake runners make, regardless of age and gender, is running easy days too hard. I can’t over-emphasize this (having committed the same mistake myself.) Be brutally honest when trying to determine what your easy pace really is. Another common mistake is skipping cutback weeks. It might feel like running more is the fastest way to get faster, but, even putting aside injury, your body physically needs cutback weeks in order to progress - your body doesn’t get faster during your runs, it gets faster when you let it absorb the training. The other one relates to fueling - many teenage runners want to maintain a good body image, so skip eating. But eating more doesn’t mean getting heavier - it means you’re responsible, providing your body the energy it needs (and burns off) so as to not crash and burn. It’s worth noting that if something feels off, it may be worth paying a quick visit to the doctor or dietician - RED-S (relative energy deficiency) is a risk teens that are increasing training volume face due to a lack of proper fueling.
